One Dial or Many?
Mapping caution inside Gemma-3-4B

When a chatbot refuses, is there a single internal "caution dial" that gets turned up, or several separate mechanisms? We opened up Google's Gemma-3-4B, found the part of its internal activity that controls refusal, and then removed it, amplified it and pushed it with different instructions to find out.

Short answer: one dial for "no", a second route for "not quite".

How a model "decides" to refuse

A language model reads your message one token (word piece) at a time. At every layer, each token is represented by a list of 2,560 numbers, the model's internal "state". Researchers found that refusal shows up as one particular direction in that 2,560-dimensional space (Arditi et al., 2024). How far a prompt's state points along that direction is the dial: far along means "this looks harmful, refuse".

  1. Find the dial. Average the internal state over many harmful prompts, average it over many harmless ones, and take the difference. That arrow is the refusal direction ("Direction A").
  2. Remove it ("abliteration"). At every layer, subtract the part of the state that points along the arrow. If refusal really lives there, the model stops refusing. We use this as our causal test.
  3. Add it ("steering"). Push a harmless prompt's state along the arrow. If the model now refuses "Give an example of an Arctic animal", the arrow really causes refusal.

Our question: is this one arrow the whole story? Different kinds of danger (violence, privacy, fraud…), and a safety instruction added to the prompt, might each use their own dial.

What we did

The model

Google Gemma-3-4B-it, in full 32-bit precision on two free Kaggle T4 GPUs. 34 layers, 2,560 numbers per token. Everything is forward passes and generation: no training.

The prompts

OR-Bench Toxic (655 harmful), Alpaca (600 harmless), and OR-Bench Hard-1K (1,319 prompts that sound risky but are fine, e.g. "how do I kill a Python process"). Every set is split: half to find directions, half to test them.

The instructions

Gemma has no system prompt, so instructions are placed before the user's question. The main manipulation is a strict safety rule, compared with a length-matched neutral one, plus 14 others: personas, milder rules, "no disclaimers"…

Research question 1

Is there one dial, or one per kind of danger?

We computed a separate refusal direction for each of 9 harm categories and asked two things. Do they point the same way? And does the part that is unique to a category (its "residue", what is left after removing the shared dial) cause refusal on its own?

FindingEvery category direction makes harmless prompts refuse, but its residue never does (0–1%, the same as random noise). The categories differ geometrically (the residues carry information about topic), but the refusal itself runs through one shared dial.

Causal test: which part of the instruction's push does the work?

We split the instruction's average effect on the internal state into the part along the dial (only of its size) and the part off the dial. Then we subtracted each part from strict prompts, or added it to neutral ones, at layer 17.

FindingThe instruction mostly pushes off the dial: the angle between its push and the dial gives a cosine of only , and prompts move just ~9% of the harmless-to-harmful distance. Yet the tiny on-dial part does about half the work. The instruction partly turns the dial and partly lowers the bar for refusing.
Research questions 4 & 5

What survives when the dial is removed?

The decisive test. We removed the dial at every layer and generated real replies to 150 borderline prompts under four instructions: neutral, the strict safety rule, and two personas with no safety rule (a children's librarian and a bank compliance officer). The blind judge labelled all 1,200 replies.

Extra refusal compared with the neutral instruction, with 95% confidence intervals (bootstrap over prompts).

FindingRemoving the dial deletes almost every lecture and flat refusal (2 of 600 benign replies remain; none on harmful prompts). But about a third of the over-caution caused by the strict rule or the librarian persona survives, and all of it as redirection: "here's the general idea, but I won't give specifics". The dial is the model's "no" button; polite dodging has a second route.

Research question 3

Can the dial predict how cautious an instruction makes the model?

If over-caution ran through one dial, instructions that push prompts further along it should cause more over-refusal. We tested 16 instructions (safety rules, cautious and neutral personas, permissive prompts), with 100 judged replies each.

Dial position measured at:
safety rulecautious personaneutral persona / stylepermissive or reference (hollow)

FindingNo reliable relation across instructions: one wording of the safety rule barely moves the dial yet refuses 96%, and "answer concisely" moves it backwards yet adds refusals. But within any single instruction, the dial position tells which prompts get refused (AUROC ). So the dial reads how risky the prompt looks; the instruction sets how much risk is tolerated, and that setting lives mostly elsewhere.

Rigour

  • Held-out testing: every direction is found on one half of the prompts and tested on the other half.
  • Controls everywhere: random vectors of the same size, a length-matched neutral instruction, label-shuffled baselines, bootstrap confidence intervals.
  • A Gemma-3 trap we found and fixed: about 46 of the 2,560 numbers are huge (~10,000) at every position. The standard recipe puts up to 97% of the dial's weight on them, and removing it then breaks the model. Masking those numbers fixed it (model change on harmless prompts: KL 30 → 0.02).
  • Validated measurement: the fast first-word score was checked against full replies, and found to miss redirects. So every behavioural claim on this page uses judged replies.

Limitations

  • One model (Gemma-3-4B), with the dial measured mainly at one layer (17).
  • The judge agrees with a human 86% of the time on refused-vs-not, checked on only 29 replies; finer style labels are subjective (59% exact agreement).
  • OR-Bench's "benign" labels were made by AI models; some prompts are genuinely borderline.
  • Replies are greedy (no sampling) and capped at 200 tokens.
